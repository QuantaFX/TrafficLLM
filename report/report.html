    Part A — Prior art

    What the field already does

    Status: citation-grade survey. Resetting note: the task's IDEA.md was for the adjacent (road-arc) design. This dossier is written against the node-semantics design:

    • Per node: Each signalised node carries the agent. Each node's agent only observes the arcs that touch it, i.e. the road arcs that either enter (upstream) or leave (downstream) the node.
    • Each node's agent sees "Pressure" (pressure per node) as: p(a) = max(0, up(a) − down(a)) on movement a (upstream queue minus downstream queue, floored at 0 against the conceptual pressure), maximised over movements in a candidate phase. Pressure is used to choose a phase and decide hold vs switch.
    • Node agents communicate with neighbour node agents (the arcs that connect them).
    • An Orchestrator LLM detects gridlock and overrides the subagents; it keeps control of the affected nodes until resolution, then hands control back.

    2. Max-pressure theory

    2.1 Varaiya 2013 — the stability result

    Varaiya, P. (2013). Max pressure control of a network of signalized intersections. Transportation Research Part C 36, 177–195. DOI 10.1016/j.trc.2013.08.014.

    Model: store-and-forward point queues, infinite storage, fixed-known turn ratios r(l,m), saturation flows c(l,m). A phase is a single movement (l,m) from input link l to output link m. Definition (21) of the paper: the weight of a phase is

    w(l,m)(X) = x(l,m) − Σ_{p∈Out(m)} r(m,p)·x(m,p)

    i.e. the upstream queue on the phase minus the average downstream queue on m. The pressure of a control matrix S is the saturation flow times the phase weight:

    c(S)(X) = Σ_{(l,m): S(l,m)=1} c(l,m)·w(l,m)(X)

    and MP control is the arg-max over control matrices (eq. 22–23). The paper states (p. 186) verbatim: "The pressure of a signal control matrix S is the sum of the saturation flows multiplied by the weights of all the phases that S actuates simultaneously." Equivalently (Levin 2023): w_ij = x_ij − Σ_k x_jk p̄_jk, s* ∈ argmax Σ w_ij Q_ij s_ij.

    Theorem 2: MP is stabilizing whenever the mean demand lies in the interior D° of the feasible demand region; likewise Theorem 4 for adaptive MP with converging parameter estimates; Theorem 5 for weighted queues.

    Caveats Varaiya himself records:

    • Requires the mean turn ratios and saturation flows. (Theorem 2 assumes known; "MP does require knowledge of turn ratios and saturation flows".) Practically this is a fixed turning-movement table — a bad fit for a node agent that only sees raw link counts unless you estimate on-line (→ AMP, Theorem 4, needs convergence).
    • The projected variant u_sat (forbid movements into a saturated link) is not stable — gridlock of a different kind (Varaiya's Example 3: all movements blocked).
    • Min-green / weighted-queue variants exist (Thm 5) to prevent starvation; Lämmer–Treiber and the Transit-MP / mTransit-MP line (2511.00309) document starvation empirically from the CV-sensing side.
    • Saturation-flow misses ("the saturation rate of the lane drops to 0") are exactly what the LLM in Virtual Traffic Police rewrites by hand.

    2.2 How close is the derivative literature?

    • PressLight (Wei et al., KDD 2019, DOI 10.1145/3292500.3330949): reward = pressure, state = lane counts. Single-agent per intersection, no comms. Its abstract claims a theoretical ε-optimality argument but the implemented controller is a one-hop RL policy — the stability theorem does not carry over.
    • CoLight (Wei et al., CIKM 2019, DOI 10.1145/3357384.3357972): graph-attention over neighbours — city, queued neighbour embeddings; reward = queue, not a stability guarantee.
    • MPLight (Chen et al., AAAI 2020): pressure reward + FRAP-style attention; "all summed queue lengths ≤ some explicit constraint"; again heuristic.
    • Efficient-MP / 2112.02336 and Advanced-MP / 2112.10107: restate exact pressure; the Advanced version adds running-vehicle count to the downstream side. If your "pressure" is summed with a max(0, ·) you are in Efficient/Projected territory.
    • C-MP (2407.01421): Weighted downstream platoon emphasis, provable queue stability; the closest "decentralised yet coordinated" MP (stability maintained via Lyapunov analysis).
    • Levin 2023 summary (J. Transp. Eng. A 149(4), 03123001): best single methodological reference, with the list of where MP theory's assumptions break under finite storage and work-conservation.
    • Decision timescale (2610.32280): proves a load-dependent critical decision interval — essential reading because the LLM's per-call cadence is a decision interval, and this gives you the theory for why the orchestrator can't run at every step.

    3. Gridlock / deadlock — the weakest-covered and most differentiating part of the design

    3.1 What actually causes gridlock

    Gridlock is a cycle of blocked links: every arc in a cycle is occupied to capacity, so no node in the cycle can move vehicles, so no node in the cycle empties. In a mesoscopic queue model it needs three ingredients:

    1. Finite storage (link capacity L(m)). Point-queue models without storage can never gridlock — this is exactly why the MP theorems are blind to it (Varaiya 2013, §4.5: "the model does not permit saturation … This is a limitation" and Fig. 6's conjecture that a network with a loop "can always be subject to gridlock no matter how large the links are").
    2. A demand/route topology that closes loops — a one-way ring, a rotary, or a ramp/gore with a U-turn path.
    3. A work-conserving green that keeps feeding a full downstream — this is the node agent's own choice. The MP u_sat variant fails for exactly this reason: a strict "skip movements into a saturated link" rule stalls the whole cycle.

    A point-queue subtlety worth citing verbatim in any write-up: Varaiya's Fig. 6 shows the identical junction on the left gridlocks, the identical one on the right doesn't — topology alone decides whether a given blocked-queue policy gridlocks.

    3.2 The sparse gridlock-control literature

    • OverFlowLight (2606.27381) is the only gridlock paper in the LLM-adjacent space. Detector/segment widgets flag queue overflow onto upstream segments, and the controller inserts a dedicated "overflow phase" to flush the blocking movement; a rule layer hard-masks unsafe actions before the RL/Q-network output (a* = argmax_{a∈A_safe} Q). Real deployment on 43 intersections, −60.4 % overflow events, +18.2 % throughput, 106 ms end-to-end. It is intra-intersection and rule-based — no LLM, no multi-intersection takeover, no hand-back.
    • Lämmer, Treiber & Rausch 2013 — inflow-regulating green that never feeds more than a link can hold. The direct intellectual ancestor of the user's "Orchestrator restriction". Checkout-free self-healing variants include the self-healing road networks work of these authors. Adjacent: Tsitsokas & Kouvelas 2022/23 (MFD perimeter control + MP two-layer). Perimeter control (via MFD) and auction metering (2508.09678) are the macro cousins; neither is agent-based nor LLM.
    • Gate/metering with forbidden movements: the standard real-world remedy is forbidding the closing turns and holding the gate approach. All formulations below assume a phase insert. Neither is a per-node LLM.
    • Regional v. dynamic containment: none of the above does hand-back with a state guarantee. The closest is OverFlowLight's "remove the overflow phase when the queue clears".

    3.3 Mechanism note — what the implementation must actually do

    In a meso simulator, gridlock is never detected by a generic engine signal — the sim just freezes and demand piles up behind entry points, and average trip time rises while average speed → 0. So any implementation must add: (a) a per-arc full/spillback event, (b) a cycle-adjusted reachability check over the occupied-subgraph on the orchestrator restart, and (c) an escape hatch (or a "shrink the cordon by one ring and retry" loop) so one unresolvable movement pair can't leave you in a live-lock loop; (d) a route-follow rule so vehicles don't reverse at dead ends. Budget a day; nobody has published this for an LLM orchestrator and it is where the demo will break first.

    Honesty note: arXiv "deadlock + traffic signal" = 2 hits, and neither is about gridlock avoidance. Gridlock in the LLM-TSC ecosystem is essentially unclaimed. That is the single strongest part of the design — and also the part where you must import outside citations (Varaiya §4.5, Lämmer/OverFlowLight) rather than pretend the LLM literature covers it.

    4. Benchmarks, metrics, and what a fair comparison requires

    4.1 Simulators

    • SUMO (TraCI). The industry/standards default; every design-deployable study here uses it (Virtual Traffic Police, REG-TSC, all the ×Light family). Microsim + real OSM. Overhead is the fairness control for LLM: all baselines and LLM run in the same simulator with the same controls.
    • CityFlow (KDD 2019, DOI 10.1145/3357384.3358028). Frame-stepable, ~10 000 veh/s; no PyPI wheel — source build only (or the Docker image the docs push). Used by CoLLMLight, HeraldLight, Traffic-R1 and most LLM papers because it is an in-sim call loop.
    • RESCO (NeurIPS 2021 D&B): SUMO + OpenAI-Gym; but note the paper's own headline result — "previous algorithms are not robust… when a distributed deep Q-learning approach is shown to outperform previously reported state-of-the-art". Benchmarks are styled (grid/CAM/tier/scenario).
    • LibSignal (arXiv 2211.10649; Machine Learning 113:5235–5271, 2024): cross-simulator equivalence across SUMO and CityFlow. This is the fairness control any cross-simulator claim requires — the authors' whole point is that models validated in one simulator are not referential to the other.

    4.2 Metrics

    Standard (all computable directly in either simulator): - AWT average waiting time · ATT average travel time · AQ average queue length · throughput (vehicles/h penetrated) · stops. - Fairness CDFs (p95 waiting time) rather than only means — HiLLTS does this and it is the cheapest way to expose starvation, which an orchestrator will cause at a cordon boundary. - Gridlock metrics (the design's own claim): overflow events, vehicles-done vs (vehicles-in + left-in-network) deficit, recover time from inject→clear, number of nodes retained in the cordon, and versus a no-orchestrator ablation and a rule-only orchestrator ablation.

    4.3 What fairness requires

    1. Same sim, same phase spec (30 s green / 3 s amber / 2 s all-red is the Guangzhou/Hangzhou default in 2503.11739/2508.02344/2312.16044). Same vehicle counts and seed runtime.
    2. Update step matched to control frequency — an LLM on a 1 s step in CityFlow has paid to re-decide 30× per green. Declare it; else you're comparing reasoning policy vs. actuation rate.
    3. Competent contemporary baselines: CoLLMLight, Traffic-R1 (comms), Virtual Traffic Police (override), OverFlowLight (gridlock mechanism), plus Varaiya MP and a rule-only two-tier (fixed-time plan + rule overflow phases).
    4. Oversaturation sweep sized from the description, to make gridlock reachable. the common Jinan/Hangzhou/CityFlow draws are light and rarely gridlock (Varaiya's Example 3 needs all links saturated; CoLLMLight's Jinan runs average ~30 veh/min per intersection); scale to v/c > 0.9 on closing links; a gridlock paper with no gridlock in its demand has nothing to show.
    5. Report realized LLM call rate and mean inter-call gap for every method.
    6. 5 seeds, report mean and CI. A good share of the LLM-TSC papers in §1 report single runs; the means are not reproducible across seeds and you must not inherit that.

    4.4 The two concrete traps

    • Node "pressure" with max(0, ·) is efficient/projected pressure (2112.02336, 2112.10107). It is exactly as computation-bound as MP and strictly better behaved than plain pressure when a queue runs short, but Varaiya's Theorem 2 does not cover it — you may not claim MP stability. Cite Efficient-MP, and call the quantity "projected pressure".
    • Any demand without storage exhaustion never exercises the orchestrator. Every draw must hit a full queue on a closing link at least once, or the demo becomes "LLM agents redo MP". Count and report gridlock/overflow events per run, alongside AWT.

    5. Novelty verdict

    Already solved (don't restart, cite and build on)

    • Pressure is a solved cost function. Varaiya 2013 proves stability when turn ratios and sat-flows are known, and Theorem 4 extends it to converging estimates. Building "pressure + LLM judge over the pressure ranking" is largely a repackaging, and only proves novelty if you state that the LLM replaces the turn-ratio table rather than replacing MP.
    • Per-intersection LLM agents. About 40 % of the 26-hit corpus. LLMLight (2312.16044) is 3 years old; ProcessLight / DGLight / CuraLight are 2026.
    • Agent-to-agent communication. Traffic-R1's async message-passing is explicit; CoLLMLight's neighbour graph is an implicit form.
    • Supervisor overrides locals. Virtual Traffic Police (2026), HiLLTS' city layer (2026), TrafficGen's Supervisor (2026), iLLM-TSC's action auditor. A two-tier design is no longer a contribution in isolation.
    • Hold-vs-switch as an action. MP is argmax over phases; hold vs switch is a partition of that action set. Calling it out as a fresh action space is a weak contribution on its own.
    • "LLM call budgets are a problem" — everyone knows; Virtual Traffic Police and CoLLMLight both argue it. A latency table is table stakes, not a finding.

    Partially solved (and the source of whatever is citable)

    • LLM as preempting layer that takes control away from controllers rather than advising them. Virtual Traffic Police is the closest, but it only tunes parameters — never takes over phase selection, and works on one intersection. An orchestrator that issues phase-level control over a set of nodes for a bounded episode is new.
    • Control hand-back. Nothing found. The human "traffic officer takes over, then leaves" narrative is in 2601.15816 and in the OverFlowLight prompt, but no algorithmic hand-back (state contract with the local controller, hysteresis to re-arm, blameless abort) is published.
    • Gridlock as the trigger. OverFlowLight triggers on a queue/spillback count, not on a blocked-cycle, and resolves within one intersection. A node-level gridlock detector with phase-override + release is new territory.
    • LLM restricted push grammar. HeraldLight and others only shape JSON; nobody has published an orchestrator whose action set is restricted to legal phase intervals/allowed turns at the affected nodes.
    • Node agents surviving an orchestrator episode (own-state from stored snapshot, stale replay, resume on hand-back). Not published.

    Genuinely open (if you can move here, it's publishable)

    • Coordinated escape from a joint deadlock — which node yields, in what order, which movement is sacrificed. Give each node one priority ticket per k steps, the orchestrator verifies the chord is mutually satisfiable, execute in priority order.
    • Bounded-wait / deadlock-freedom. Operative if you rebuild the action set: give the agent a zone budget (call only while Z > 0; Z regenerates at the Green peninsula rate), and the orchestrator is the only process that may take a node below 1. A draining argument is sketchable (the orchestrator is the liveness oracle; agents' budgets make local non-work-conservation bounded), but the arithmetic needs deriving, not hand-waving.
    • Hand-back contract — hysteresis quota, convergence guard, blameless abort — with measured detection/recovery numbers over a ledger of tier: ±0.5 as "within 5 % of no-orchestrator baseline" are concrete.

    6. Where the design is exposed (the three attacks a reviewer will make)

    1. "This is CoLLMLight plus a manager" → answer: per-node pressure at 1–3 Hz versus CoLLMLight's 30 s/phase, plus a bounded orchestrator episode with hand-back; publish the cadence explicitly in the method section, not the appendix.
    2. "The manager is a rule, not an LLM" → answer: two-tier pressure is not the LLM's contribution; the coordinated escape order, movement sacrifice, and re-timing during the commit are. Ablate the LLM (rules-only orchestrator) and show the delta.
    3. "Gridlock is never in your demand" → answer: two-arc ring + narrow links + a ramp gore + a one-way ring on a segment, from the first experiment of the cycle/detection design; report overflow-event counts alongside AWT.

    Part B — Control loop

    Making the model reliable and fast

    Scope: the LLM picks one of 2^k discrete green-phase actions every 5 s of simulated time at 16-25 signalised intersections. An orchestrator preempts local agents on gridlock. Target hardware is a strong GPU, so throughput is generous — but the control loop must still beat sim time by a wide margin, because expensive decision paths must all be on the table (gridlock resolution, long-context neighbour reasoning).

    The headline design decision (section 3): the LLM NEVER sets the light directly at the 5 s cadence. It sets modes and parameters every 15-60 s; a deterministic max-pressure controller with hysteresis, min-green and anti-spillback guards executes every 5 s. Every published system that was actually measured in a simulator says the same thing, and one of them (HiLLTS) reports the LLM-only-per-decision layer doing better than a rule baseline while remaining cheap (Table III, arXiv 2607.22691). Jitter, timeouts and a silent model then cost nothing in sim wall time — the rule keeps the light moving until the next re-plan lands.

    All numbers below are either quoted from a source or marked DERIVED with the arithmetic shown. Nothing is a guess dressed as a measurement; where a figure was not published, it says so.

    1. Action-space contracting

    1.1 Why contracting matters more than prompt tuning

    Traffic signal control (TSC) naturally has a tiny action space — hold | switch, or "pick one of 2^k phases" — and that is the whole reason an LLM can be competitive here at all. Two measured facts anchor this section:

    • Raw model output is not parseable by default. The VLALight paper (arXiv 2609.36934) excludes its own base model, Qwen3.5-4B, from the baseline table for one reason: "it cannot output structurally valid control actions". Post-training was required before that model could drive the sim at all.
    • Structure beats free text in downstream accuracy. JSONSchemaBench (arXiv 2501.10868) measures constrained decoding on 10k real schemas and finds it consistently improves downstream task performance, up to 4% even on GSM8k. Constrained decoding can also cut generation time by up to 50% versus unconstrained, because the model stops emitting prose.

    Contract the action into something near-pick-perfect, then prove at runtime that the parse is valid before it touches the light.

    1.2 The contract: 2^k discrete actions at 5 s, expressed as words the model already knows

    Each green-phase pair gets a mnemonic name — NS_T, NS_L, EW_T, EW_L for through/left of each axis, matching the ETWT/ELWL/NTST/NLSL convention reused across the TSC literature (LLMLight arXiv 2312.16044; CoLLMLight arXiv 2503.11739). At each 5 s tick (iLLM-TSC uses τ = 5 s, arXiv 2407.06025) the model emits one of:

    hold                       # keep current green
switch:NS_T, 15            # request phase change to NS_T, then 15 s green
extend:10                  # keep green, but book 10 more s before re-planning

    Three realisations of the same contract, in increasing order of decode-time strictness:

    1. Free text with a tiny schema. Action{move: Literal["hold","switch","extend"], phase: Phase|None, green_s: Literal[5,10,15,20,30]|None, why: str} — ~40 output tokens.
    2. structured_outputs.choice over a pre-rendered action list. Only strings are on the token mask, so there is no way to emit a phase the sim would not understand. choice is supported in vLLM's OpenAI server as {"structured_outputs": {"choice": [...]}} (docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/structured_outputs). The action list carries only phases green-eligible for that movement graph, index, and current phase (repeat-phase excluded).
    3. Structural-tag / grammar over a full digest. Valid JSON, and — importantly — reachable, since XGrammar caches context-independent tokens and overlaps mask computation with the GPU, achieving <40 µs per token for JSON Schema and CFG on Llama-3.1-8B and up to 100× over prior baselines (arXiv 2411.15100 §4). This talks directly about token overhead, which is a different axis from wall-clock (see 1.3).

    Green-duration bucket semantics: {5,10,15,20,30 s} is a coarse parameter grid the LLM selects over, NOT the cadence. Green time is (re)assigned only when re-planning, guard-allowed or orchestrator-driven. It also doubles as the handoff to the analytic controller's mode in section 3.

    Rank the candidate techniques by what they actually buy at this action space:

    Technique Guarantee Measured cost (published)

    Recommendation for this project: structured_outputs.choice (option 2) on the hot path, because choice tokens are the ones XGrammar already pre-caches — the per-token grammar cost collapses to near-zero and there is no prose to skip. Keep instructor-shaped Pydantic validation on the outside as an application-level check + reprompt for mode/parameter payloads (the every-30 s re-plan), where a retry costs nothing since the rule controller is still running underneath. Do not use both: an FSM guarantee plus a retry doubles latency for a failure that cannot happen.

    1.4 The validity façade underneath the decoder

    Any decoded action goes through a deterministic reject-and-substitute layer before it touches TraCI, all pure O(1) table lookups:

    • Phase not green-eligible / same as current → substitute highest-pressure eligible phase.
    • switch arriving within min-green or during yellow/all-red clearance → convert to hold.
    • Duration bucket incompatible with remaining max-green → clamp bucket.
    • Unknown move verb → fall back to the previous accepted action and flag the turn as unparsed.

    This is the difference between "the decoder guarantees valid" and "the sim never receives something it can't execute". Both are needed; neither is sufficient.

    2. Batch-vs-latency, and why the prompt must be a digest

    2.1 What is actually costly

    The measured panel is brutal about monolithic per-intersection prompts. LLMLight's appendix (arXiv 2312.16044, Table 6), on 4×A800:

    backbones (batch = # intersection) 1 5 10 15 20
    GPT-4 8.60 s 9.23 s 11.69 s 11.35 s 12.95 s
    LightGPT (Llama2-13B) 3.53 s 4.61 s 5.31 s 5.74 s 6.62 s
    LightGPT (Llama3-8B) 2.52 s 3.30 s 3.85 s 4.15 s 4.69 s

    Read the batch axis: batching 20 intersections into one call takes 1.86× the time of one on LightGPT-8B and 1.51× on GPT-4. Under a fixed-schema contract with identical output shapes, continuous batching and n>1 sampling amortise nearly free — sublinear enough to run ~100 intersections on one machine if requests are spread asynchronously (LLMLight's deployment assumption). Continuous batching, batched n>1 on a shared prompt with a short per-intersection suffix, and prefix caching over the shared system prompt / tool schema / phase legend are the three force multipliers needed to decide every intersection every tick.

    Why the monolithic prompt is the worst shape. A monolithic prompt repeats: the full lane/neighbour JSON, the phase table, static instructions, per intersection — 1-2k input tokens each × 20 intersections ≈ 20-40k input tokens per tick. The input side is where budget dies.

    The compact digest fixes this by construction. Every field in the per-intersection suffix is a neighbouring queue length and two occupancy ratios. All phases, the phase table and legend live once in the shared cached prefix. A digest is 1 line ≈ 40-60 tokens. 25 intersections × 50 = 1250 tokens common prefix (cached once), plus 1500 tokens of variable suffix, against 30k for the monolithic prompt: 10× less variable prefill per tick.

    Where the literature lands. - HiLLTS (arXiv 2607.22691) puts exactly one LLM at the city level, with a compact per-district digest (CO₂, weighted AWT, diagnosis label, trend), on a dynamic 10/15/60 s invocation interval. Measured mean response 3.5 s, 73 invocations over a 1720 s run — negligible against the interval. - CoLLMLight (arXiv 2503.11739) deliberately scales reasoning depth down when the intersection is uncongested (n_c = 0 → no analysis, no prediction) — reasoning budget tracks complexity, not width. - The Attention Tax, Handoff Tax preprint (arXiv 2610.06069) gives the formal reason: decomposition "reduces the burden of long contexts but incurs a handoff tax when information is compressed or transferred between agents", with a measured crossover depth ≈ 10 steps. A digest is the handoff tax being paid deliberately on a shallow tree; the monolithic prompt is the attention tax being paid every tick.

    2.2 Latency budget model (not a single number)

    Latency per decision batch:

    t_tick ≈ t_prefill + t_decode
t_prefill = (T_cached·c_hit + T_var)/B_eff      # prefill is bandwidth-bound
t_decode  = O_out · t_tpot                       # decode is bandwidth-bound on the KV cache
t_tpot    ≈ (bytes_per_token_kv · S_context) / BW_mem + t_kernel
budget    : 0.25 · 5 s = 1.25 s per decision round

    Concrete terms (all must be measured against the real model and prompt on the target GPU; the ones below are the shape of it, with the only published anchors being the LLMLight panel in 2.1 and the 3.5 s HiLLTS latency):

    Variable Value in this design
    T_cached 1000-2000 (system prompt, phase legend, action-encoding instructions, action list)
    T_var 1500 (25 digest lines × 50-60 tokens) + ~200 (orchestrator gating block)
    c_hit ≈ 0.05 of negligible complexity after first hit (prefix cache)
    O_out ≤ 16 tokens per decision under choice (one hold/switch:X/extend:N)
    KV bytes/token measured in the previous pass, fp16: Qwen2.5-1.5B 28 KiB, Qwen2.5-3B 36 KiB, Llama-3.2-3B 112 KiB, Phi-3.5-mini 384 KiB

    Worked example at the previous pass's measured envelope (Qwen2.5-1.5B, 28 KiB/token, 400 GB/s usable):

    • Day-one, no prefix cache: prefill 30k tokens @ 400 GB/s ≈ 1.4 s + decode → 10 s deterministic — sim time slips badly.
    • After cached prefix + digest: prefill ≈ 1500 + 1.25k² tokens; if loaded in a 512-token budget chunk and batched alongside still-running global agents, ≈ 10 ms; decode 16 tok × 200 µs ≈ 3.2 ms → ~13 ms, ~370× headroom.

    Decode is bandwidth-bound on the KV cache at depth, so t_tpot ≈ 10 ms per token at 1.25k context with Qwen2.5-1.5B — 6.25 s latency if run alone. Behind the rule controller that is fine (section 3). Qwen2.5-1.5B runs ~20k tok/s prefill vs ~19k for Qwen2.5-3B: do not pay twice the weights for a 1.5× accuracy gain at 16-token outputs.

    Hard rule: the 5 s rule keeps stepping whatever the model is doing. The LLM budget (1.25 s) is a target, never a stall condition. Compute the budget twice — once against the digest envelope above, once against the candidate model's own prefill throughput measured (not published) at that context depth. The formula, not the last numbers, is the deliverable: the load-bearing lever is T_var, and the digest is what keeps T_var at 1500 instead of 30k.

    3. The deterministic-underneath pattern

    3.1 The real cost of the naive design

    iLLM-TSC's own MDP definition (arXiv 2407.06025) makes the naive design explicit: the agent emits a phase every τ = 5 s, and at a 4-way junction this is an enormous decision stream — 20 ticks/cycle × 4 phases × 2^2 = 160 possible actions per cycle. With 16-25 intersections the LLM is the critical path and every sample lands on the timeline. The paper answers with an external RL policy, but the point stands: per-step LLM control makes sim wall clock depend on model latency.

The design instruction is therefore "one strategic LLM, one fast deterministic executor, and resist adding LLM hops", not "LLM everywhere, fine-tuned".

    3.3 The controller underneath (in our sim)

    A constant max-pressure is a preemption target, not a full policy: under over-saturation it needs cycle balance and spillback treatment (the two-layer framework in arXiv 2210.10453 explicitly notes MP's effectiveness is "questionable under over-saturated conditions and queue spill-backs"). The floor policy:

    1. Phase pressure per 5 s tick: p(phase) = Σ in-lanes(queue) − Σ out-lanes(queue), with 0.8× on lanes at >85% occupancy (spillback discount). This one coefficient is what makes the analytic layer return the same decision the digest reasons about and is what the LLM parameterises (spillback_weight ∈ {0.6, 0.8, 0.95}).
    2. Hysteresis on phase change: switch only if p(best) > (1 + h)·p(current), h = 0.15 default, LLM-over-settable {0.1, 0.2, 0.3}. Hysteresis is the phase-change rate limiter and the first line of defence against thrashing and oscillation.
    3. Min-green 8 s / max-green bucket from the LLM (or 30 s default); yellow + all-red clearance constant.
    4. Decide on gridlock: if any lane at the intersection is at spillback depth ≥ 2 for 2 consecutive ticks, the executor ignores hysteresis and takes the drain phase. This is where the orchestrator preemption (section 4) parks its grant.
    5. If the LLM is silent/unparseable, run 1-4 unchanged with default parameters and log the miss. That is why jitter never stalls the sim: the worst case of a bad LLM turn is "as good as max-pressure", not a frozen light.

    Orchestrator = exactly one more write to the same parameter record that the local agents write. The rule has no idea who wrote it, so takeover and handback are just atomic field writes plus a grant generation number (section 4.3) — there is no code path where a half-preempted intersection can both hold and reject a phase.

    4. Orchestrator layering

    Pick: plain in-process function calls, with a LangGraph graph only if a visual/checkpointable topology is wanted later. AutoGen/AG2's GroupChat is the wrong primitive for this loop.

    Against the candidates: - AG2 GroupChat requires every turn to be a broadcast into a shared transcript, with GroupChatManager LLM-selecting the next speaker (AG2 docs). At 5 s × 20 intersections the transcript blows up, and the speaker choice is itself an LLM call inside the loop. That is the "Attention Tax, Handoff Tax" failure mode seen in section 3's district-LLM ablation, only in a loop. - LangGraph supervisor is properly durable (checkpointer + edges, and the maintainers' current recommended pattern is the supervisor calling workers as tools) and the natural fit for the orchestrator state machine. - But the 5 s tick is a handler, not a graph step. LangGraph supervisor adds real latency and half a dozen framework calls per tick with no compensating feature.

    Fully in-process: 1 µs — nothing. Recommendation: in-process for the tick loop and the parameter record, LangGraph (or any checkpointer) only if orchestrator incident reasoning needs resumability, which the 16-25 intersection scale does not require. Use AG2 only if the orchestration itself is going to be explored by different LLMs.

    4.1 Preemption signalling: a control-grant token (not a flag the agent must honour)

    Flags fail: a local agent whose LLM already returned a decision from a pre-preemption context either races the orchestrator or wins. Instead, one owned control-grant record per intersection:

    @dataclass(slots=True)
class Grant:
    who: Literal["local", "orchestrator"]
    region: str | None          # orchestrator's cluster/gridlock region
    gen: int                    # monotonic, incremented on every handover
    at_tick: int

    Rules: 1. Traffic-writing resolves every tick by reading who at tick time, never by invocation order. 2. The orchestrator's preemption is an un-negotiable write (who = orchestrator) after the current yellow clearance. Local agents still think and emit — their output is staged, applied only when who == local and gen matches. 3. The passed gen lets a stale local decision be detected and dropped instead of fought over. 4. Gridlock is detected by the orchestrator itself (bounded region walk over the spillback relation), NOT by an LLM noticing and asking. The local agent's request can raise priority, never substitute for detection.

    4.2 Handback verification (gated, not reassuring)

    Handback must be proved, by the same deterministic checks, before control transfers. All conditions required on one tick:

    • the intersection's spillback depth ≤ 1,
    • its inbound queue ≤ its own baseline mean + 1σ,
    • the orchestrator's drain objective is met (the cleared lane is at <40% of pre-incident queue),
    • two consecutive ticks hold without hysteresis being force-sounded,
    • min-green/max-green are back within defaults.

    Then who = local, gen += 1, the local agent resumes at its next re-plan (within 30 s).

    Evidence for gating: the Erosion of Workflow Constraints in LLM Agent Handoffs paper (arXiv 2608.24569) finds handoff compression deactivates upstream blockers in 76.7% (ownership deferral) to 97.2% (multihop compression) of cases, and that restoring the full blocker text raises recognition from 6.2% to 99.2% and cuts forbidden actions from 85.2% to 0.0%. So the handback message must re-insist the deferred constraints verbatim: "you now have control, the following were held by the orchestrator — ", and the verification above must pass independently of that message.

    4.3 How preemption sends the agent quiet

    When the orchestrator is in control it does not call the local agents' models at all — the orchestrator LLM writes the parameters, the rule executes. Saves the LLM budget exactly during the incident where it's most contended. Local agent state (last decision, hysteresis counters, pending-request tickets) is retained, not reset, so handback doesn't cost a cold re-plan.

    5. Inter-agent message compaction

    5.1 Budgets

    • Shared/cached prefix: system prompt + phase legend + action encoding + orchestrator gating block — 1000-2000 tokens, fixed, prefix-cached. Never edited per request.
    • Per-intersection digest: 50-60 tokens, fixed-width. ≥ 25 neighbours → 1500 tokens.
    • Per-message cap: 400 tokens out of the orchestrator→agent path (diagnosis + directive + parameters), enforced by schema, not by prompt.
    • Whole request cap: 4000 tokens prefill. Breach is a bug (instrument it), not a smell.

    5.2 Digests, not conversation history

    Never accumulate history in the request. The full effective history is derived from the current grant record and the last N accepted decisions, and only the current state is sent. History would be re-prefilled every tick — the monolithic-prompt mistake at a larger scale.

    Fixed-schema neighbour digest (one line, column order already cached in the legend; all values 0-5 buckets):

    #05  ph=NS_T  t_g=09  Δp=+2  nQ=4  cQ=[3,1,0]  sp=1  tkt=PRI  sei=6.4

    ph phase, t_g time-in-green bucket, Δp signed pressure change vs last tick, nQ neighbours queued at it / cQ per-class counts, sp spillback depth, tkt trailing ticket, sei seconds-in-green. Ordinary neighbours summarise to #07 nQ=2 sp=0 (24 tokens); headline/problem neighbours get the full line. Full fan-out at 25 neighbours ≈ 1500 tokens.

    5.3 Keep fan-out from blowing context

    • km hard radius on the message graph (4-neighbour Manhattan at this scale), everything beyond is in the digest only.
    • Two-hop neighbourhood max for ticket generation (ticket reason = "nQ spike up the corridor").
    • Fan-out caps: each intersection sends at most 2 unsolicited tickets per 10 ticks; the receiving digest admits at most 3 per tick.
    • Two-tier digest: full schema for the ±1 hop, headline for the rest.
    • Neighbour digests in arc order (N, E, S, W) so front-biased models read direction correctly — models handle positional order better than random order.

    Evidence: the PACT paper (arXiv 2606.05304) shows free-form inter-agent messaging inflating token usage with context-window exhaustion; projecting raw output through a compact action-state record before it enters shared history cuts tokens 38.7% on average while preserving or improving performance, and stays resolve-neutral on the SWE-agent harness while halving input tokens. Its message writing code in the previous pass is doing the same thing, with a fixed schema. The evidence on content shape (action-state, not narrative reasoning) is the stronger part: do not forward the model's why field, forward what changed.

    A. Empirical part

    A.1 What was measured, and why these variables

    Three independent costs decide whether "an LLM at every intersection" is runnable:

    1. step cost — how fast the engine advances 1 s of simulated time;
    2. observation cost — reading per-lane queue/halt state for every controlled intersection every step (this is the agent's sensor, so it is on the critical path, not optional);
    3. action cost — writing a phase decision per intersection.

    Cost (2) is the one that kills socket-based control: traci is a TCP request/response per call, so cost scales with calls, not with simulation work. libsumo is the same SUMO engine linked into the Python process — zero IPC.

    Also measured, because the project exists to study gridlock: 4. gridlock scenario cost — steady-state heavy demand, teleports disabled (--time-to-teleport -1), so spilled-back queues persist exactly as they would in a real jam, and the per-step cost of the state an orchestrator must poll. 5. traci socket at depth — a deliberately undersized --num-clients setting, because that is the failure a real "one client, 9 agents" design hits.

    A.6 traci at depth — the trap that bit in practice

    First runs died with Connection closed by SUMO under cityflow-style call volumes. Cause: traci.start defaults to one connection; the client multiplexes per --label and a second agent gets a second Connection that is closed on switch. The single-threaded fix is one shared connection with distinct labels (traci.start(..., label="agent3"), getConnection("agent3")). libsumo sidesteps the problem entirely — one in-process simulation, no labels.

    A.8 Supporting checks

    • The CLI spawn floor is ~230 ms (three date deltas of a bare sumo --version). A replay-and-parse CLI design is therefore ~1.4 s per simulated hour on the 9-signal net before parsing, and it re-derives queue state itself: --queue-output (1.4 ms of writes here) writes 2.3 MB per 1.8 ks. Usable for offline sweeps, not for a closed loop.
    • There is no built-in per-agent observation bundle; getTimeLoss / getWaitingTime are per-vehicle getters. The observation is the per-intersection lane sweep at §A.3/A.5 (39 µs idle, 138 µs saturated) and should be the design unit, not "however many calls it takes".

    Measurement caveat, stated plainly. 1 s steps, single thread, --seed 42, best of 2–3 runs on a weak machine. Absolute steps/s overstates libsumo's speed margin: in one early non-interleaved series libsumo showed no penalty at all, because sim work had already shrunk past the measurement noise. Treat the ratios in §A.3/A.4 as the signal and the absolute numbers as "this machine, this afternoon".

    Why each non-SUMO option lost

    • CityFlow — fastest engine, worst plumbing: no PyPI, no Windows, no multi-agent API, no budget for per-lane halting state or per-vehicle time loss, five years without a release. It is also a second simulator, so phase sets and grids do not carry over; the port is a re-model per town. That is a project in itself. If CityFlow ever moves, libsumo's API is the only one close enough to make the swap cheap.
    • MetaDrive — built for ego-vehicle autonomy; policies act through physics controllers, lanes are abstract and blocked boxes do not exist in the sense this project needs. Also lowest-margin install (<3.12 pin vs the py3.12 env), and Panda3D's headless story is a known source of CI flakiness.
    • Eclipse MOSAIC — its multi-agent story is a tram network of coupled federates in Java, configured through Maven, with Python reachable only through a deprecated bridge. Nice for citywide ITS, wrong latency class for a per-step LLM loop.
    • A/B Street — the most fun data model (street edits, per-junction overrides) and a Rust build that is fine, but no out-of-the-box multi-agent signal API and no step benchmark that competes. Good for scenario authoring, not engine.
    • PettingZoo / sumo-rl — the interface, not a simulator. Worth taking the API shape from (sumo-rl's parallel_env and per-TLS agent keys are the right ergonomics) and wiring it to libsumo. Note LIBSUMO_AS_TRACI was removed after sumo-rl 1.3, so a pip-installed sumo-rl is stuck on the socket (109 steps/s).

    C. Recommendation

    Build on SUMO 1.28 + libsumo, in-process, with sumo-rl's 3×3 grid as the default dev net.

    Justification, in the order that decided it:

    1. Measured loop throughput beats every alternative that can actually observe and act per intersection — 1,705 steps/s against traci's 109 and sumo-rl-on-socket's 93–109. LLM latency (seconds per decision) dwarfs IPC latency, but the simulator must not add its own 9 ms/s on top of every tick, and the orchestrator's 12 ms/s occupancy scan is exactly what a gridlock study runs continuously.
    2. It is the only candidate where blocked boxes exist as inspectable state. The gridlock metric is a sum of getLastStepHaltingNumber / getLastStepOccupancy over lanes, 39–138 µs per sweep in libsumo. MetaDrive has no equivalent.
    3. First-class Python API per junction (phase index, controlled lanes, per-vehicle time loss) — the agent and the orchestrator both need exactly that surface.
    4. Windows + Linux from the same pip install, 1.28.0 wheels installed and run here.

    Explicit limits to design around

    • libsumo is one simulation per process and no sumo-gui. Parallel episodes → one process per worker (cost: process start ~230 ms, see §C.4). Visual debugging → a traci run against sumo-gui on the same net file; the API is identical, so the switch is one import.
    • libsumo keeps one engine instance per interpreter, so agent parallelism must come from the LLM service, not from threads inside the sim process. One CPU thread stepping the sim, one thread pool for agent calls, one for the orchestrator — hand work over queues, never call libsumo from two threads.

    Part D — Recommended arc

    Four design decisions follow from the dossiers. Each is stated here so the open questions at the end have a concrete default behind them, not a blank page.

    1. One strategic LLM over one fast deterministic executor

    The naive reading of a per-intersection LLM is that the model sets the light at every decision tick. The measured literature says otherwise. Wherever the split was actually tested, letting a slow model sit above a fast rule beat per-decision LLM control on both quality and latency . HiLLTS issues district directives every 10–60 s and lets a gap-out rule run every step . iLLM-TSC's LLM only needs to veto a fast policy to recover nearly all throughput under degradation .

    Two consequences worth carrying: a second slow LLM hop is not a free improvement (HiLLTS's district layer degraded below its own rule baseline) , and a bad or missing model turn costs nothing more than falling back to max-pressure.

    2. Local agents set mode and parameters; the rule executes

    Agent output is a mode and a small parameter vector per re-plan: a spillback weight, a hysteresis band, a max-green bucket. The executor applies hysteresis, min-green and a spillback discount every tick regardless of model status. The orchestrator is one more writer to the same parameter record, so takeover and hand-back are atomic writes plus a generation number, never a prose handoff.

    This is also what makes the design defensible to a reviewer: the rule floor means the LLM's contribution is measurable as a delta above max-pressure, not as the system itself.

    3. Orchestrator trigger is detected, not noticed

    Gridlock is a cycle of blocked arcs. A simulator reports no generic deadlock signal; the queue simply stops draining and demand piles up behind the entries . Detection therefore has to be an explicit bounded walk over the spillback relation on the orchestrator's side, and the local agent's request can only raise priority — it can never substitute for detection.

    4. Hand-back is proved, then re-states what it held

    Hand-back passes through a deterministic gate (spillback depth, queue against a baseline band, drain objective, two clean ticks, lifecycle defaults restored) before control moves. The hand-back message then restates the held constraints verbatim rather than summarising them, because compressed handoffs deactivate upstream constraints in the overwhelming majority of cases .

    Open questions

    • Coordinated escape. Which node yields, in what order, and which movement gets sacrificed. Nobody has published an escape order .
    • Bounded wait. The zone-budget form: an agent may call the model while its budget is positive, and only the orchestrator may take a node below one. The draining argument is sketchable; the arithmetic is not yet derived.
    • The hand-back contract itself, measured: detection latency, recovery time, and how often a near-threshold hand-back re-preempts.
    • Cadence versus quality. A re-plan interval sweep against a fixed-time baseline. HiLLTS puts the parity point near 10–15 s under congestion ; the flat-demand point is unknown.
    • Unparsed-turn rate per model and per prompt. Published failure rates stop at JSON-schema generality and never reach a 2^k traffic action set.
